Turkey Burger Recipe: Juicy and Safe at 165°F

Buy 93% lean ground turkey, add a panko-butter-Parmesan panade, and cook to 165°F (74°C) on a thermometer: a turkey burger that stays juicy.

By Clara, Recipes editorPublished November 24, 2026

A juicy turkey burger comes down to three things. Buy 93% lean ground turkey, not 99%. Work in a panade of panko, melted butter, soy sauce and grated Parmesan so the lean meat holds its moisture. Then cook the patties until a thermometer reads 165°F (74°C), the USDA safe minimum for all ground poultry. In a skillet over medium heat that’s about 5 minutes per side. Don’t press them. And trust the thermometer, not the clock or the color.

Turkey burgers have a reputation for being dry, and it’s mostly earned by the meat itself. Below: why ground turkey dries out, which fat percentage to buy, the full recipe, and what the food safety agencies of the US, Canada, France and Spain actually say about doneness.

Why turkey burgers dry out

Turkey burgers dry out because ground turkey has very little fat compared with burger beef. Ground turkey can be close to 99% lean, while a good ground beef for burgers carries 20 to 30% fat (America’s Test Kitchen). In beef, that fat melts as the patty cooks and bastes it from the inside. Turkey has almost none to give. You have to put it back.

There’s a second problem: turkey has to be cooked further than beef. The USDA safe minimum is 165°F for ground poultry and 160°F for ground beef. Pulling the patty early isn’t an option, so the fix has to go into the mix and the cooking method.

For the beef side of the comparison, see my guide to the best ground beef for burgers and the right fat ratio.

93% lean ground turkey for burgers: which one to buy

For burgers, buy 93% lean ground turkey (93/7) rather than 99% lean. America’s Test Kitchen recommends 93/7 because its 7% fat gives more flavor and moisture than the nearly fat-free 99% version (ATK, In Defense of Turkey Burgers). The 99% pack is all white meat. It’s the one that cooks up dry, and in my view no panade fully rescues it, so leave it on the shelf.

Ground meat Lean / fat What to expect in a burger
Ground turkey 99% lean 99 / 1 Almost no fat. Dries out easily, avoid for burgers
Ground turkey 93% lean 93 / 7 The one to buy. Enough fat for flavor and moisture with a panade
Ground beef for burgers 70–80 / 20–30 The beef reference. Its own fat keeps it juicy without additions

How to make juicy turkey burgers

To make juicy turkey burgers, add back what the lean meat lacks: a starch that holds water, a little fat and something savory. America’s Test Kitchen has published two versions of this. I prefer the first.

The panade: panko, butter, soy sauce and Parmesan

A panade is a mix of starch and liquid worked into the meat to hold on to moisture as it cooks. ATK’s per-pound formula uses 3 tablespoons of panko, unflavored gelatin, baking soda dissolved in 1 teaspoon of water, 1 tablespoon of melted butter, 1½ tablespoons of soy sauce and 3 tablespoons of grated Parmesan (ATK). The butter stands in for some of the missing fat. Soy sauce and Parmesan do the rest: lean turkey tastes of very little on its own, and those two give it the savory depth beef gets for free.

The mayo and Monterey Jack variation

ATK’s second version works panko, mayonnaise and shredded Monterey Jack into the meat. According to ATK, the mayonnaise helps the patty brown into a golden crust, and the cheese melts into pockets of fat inside (ATK, Crispy, Juicy Turkey Burgers). It’s the easier route if you don’t keep gelatin in the pantry. I still reach for the panade version first, because it tastes of turkey rather than of melted cheese.

Don’t press the patty

Never press a turkey burger with the spatula while it cooks. Pressing squeezes out the juices you just worked to keep, and on a patty this lean the loss shows right away.

Turkey burger recipe: ingredients

This turkey burger recipe uses ATK’s per-pound panade ratios and makes 4 patties of about 4 oz (113 g) each. ATK’s recipe, linked above, gives the gelatin and baking soda amounts. Everything else is listed below in US measures with metric equivalents.

  • 1 lb (454 g) 93% lean ground turkey, cold
  • 3 tbsp (45 ml) panko breadcrumbs
  • 1 tbsp (15 ml) unsalted butter, melted
  • 1½ tbsp (22 ml) soy sauce
  • 3 tbsp (45 ml) finely grated Parmesan
  • Unflavored powdered gelatin (amount per ATK’s recipe)
  • Baking soda (amount per ATK’s recipe), dissolved in 1 tsp (5 ml) water
  • Neutral oil, a thin film for the pan
  • 4 burger buns, plus the toppings you like

Turkey burger recipe step by step

Seven steps take you from cold meat to a burger checked with a thermometer. Keep an instant-read thermometer within reach: it, not the timer, decides when the burger is done.

1. Dissolve the baking soda

Stir the baking soda into 1 teaspoon (5 ml) of water until it dissolves. Undissolved grains won’t spread evenly through the meat.

2. Make the panade

In a large bowl, combine the panko, gelatin, melted butter, soy sauce, Parmesan and the baking soda solution. Stir into an even paste.

3. Mix in the turkey and shape the patties

Add the cold ground turkey and mix gently with your hands until just combined. Divide into 4 equal portions of about 4 oz (113 g) and shape them into patties of even thickness.

Four raw ground turkey patties shaped to an even thickness before cooking. This mix uses herbs and diced vegetables, not the panko panade from this recipe (2272×1704). (Photo: Miriam Kato, CC BY 2.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mar-a-Lago\_Turkey\_Burgers\_Prep\_(4330640025).jpg)

4. Start in a cold, oiled skillet

Put the patties in a cold skillet with a thin film of oil, set the heat to medium and cover with a lid. ATK uses this cold-start, covered method so the inside cooks through before the outside overcooks.

5. Cook about 5 minutes per side

Cook for about 5 minutes per side over medium heat, flipping once and never pressing down. Take that timing as a guide only. Patty thickness and your stove will move it.

6. Check for 165°F (74°C) in the center

Push the thermometer probe into the side of the thickest patty, all the way to the center. The burger is done when it reads 165°F (74°C).

7. Toast the buns and assemble

Toast the buns cut side down, then build the burgers with your toppings. Serve right away: a lean patty loses heat and juiciness fast once it leaves the pan.

Turkey burger internal temp: 165°F (74°C)

The safe internal temperature for a turkey burger is 165°F (74°C), measured in the center of the patty. The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service and FoodSafety.gov set 165°F as the safe minimum for all poultry, including ground poultry. Ground beef and pork have a lower minimum of 160°F (71°C). For every other patty, see our internal temperature chart for every burger patty.

Carryover heat: a cook’s technique, not a rule

Some cooks take turkey burgers off the heat at 161–163°F (72–73°C) and let carryover heat finish the job while they rest. That is a kitchen technique, not an official recommendation. If you do it, check that the center actually reaches 165°F (74°C) before serving. Some recipes, ATK’s included, aim for 160°F. I don’t. The USDA says 165°F, and with the panade doing its job, those extra 5 degrees cost you very little juiciness.

How long to cook turkey burgers on the stove

On the stove, turkey burgers take about 5 minutes per side over medium heat, about 10 minutes in total. That’s the figure most skillet recipes give (Izzy Cooking). It gets you close. The thermometer tells you when you’re there, and a thicker patty or a hotter burner will shift it either way.

Grilled turkey burgers

Grilled turkey burgers follow the same rules as the skillet version: a 93% lean mix with a panade, no pressing and a 165°F (74°C) reading in the center. The panade helps the patty hold together on the grates. High heat is the risk: a lean patty dries out fast over open flame, so check the temperature early, flip once, and take them off the moment the center reads 165°F.

Turkey burger vs. veggie burger

Turkey and plants are the two main routes to a burger without beef. Turkey still gives you a meat patty, with less fat, but it asks more of the cook: a panade and a thermometer. A veggie patty has no safe-temperature rule to hit. For the fully meatless version, try my homemade veggie burger recipe, or browse every base recipe and copycat on our burger recipes hub.

Turkey burger FAQ

What temperature should a turkey burger be cooked to? Cook a turkey burger to 165°F (74°C) in the center. That’s the USDA safe minimum for all ground poultry, and Health Canada uses the same 74°C. France’s ANSES and Spain’s AESAN set at least 70°C (158°F) for ground meat.

Can a turkey burger be a little pink in the middle? Color doesn’t tell you whether a turkey burger is safe. A patty can look done before it is, and a fully cooked one can still be slightly pink. Only an instant-read thermometer reading 165°F (74°C) in the center confirms it.

Why are my turkey burgers dry, and how do I keep them juicy? They’re dry because ground turkey has very little fat, sometimes only 1%. To keep them juicy, buy 93% lean, add a panade (panko, melted butter, soy sauce, Parmesan, or mayo and Monterey Jack), don’t press the patties, and take them off the heat as soon as they hit 165°F.

Should I buy 93% or 99% lean ground turkey for burgers? Buy 93% lean. America’s Test Kitchen recommends it over 99% because the 7% fat adds flavor and moisture, and burgers made with the 99% version come out dry.

How long do turkey burgers take in a skillet? About 5 minutes per side over medium heat, roughly 10 minutes in total. Patty thickness and your stove change the timing, so check for 165°F (74°C) instead of going by the clock.

Do I need egg or breadcrumbs in a turkey burger? No egg needed here. This recipe binds with a panade of panko, gelatin and Parmesan, and the panko soaks up liquid and holds it in the patty as it cooks.
