Burger Seasoning Recipe (and When to Salt)

A salt-free spice blend you mix ahead, and salt on the surface right before cooking, so patties stay tender instead of going springy like sausage.

By Clara, Recipes editorPublished November 27, 2026

The best burger seasoning comes in two parts. First, a salt-free base of smoked paprika, garlic powder, onion powder, black pepper and dry mustard that you mix once and keep in a jar. Second, salt, sprinkled on the outside of each patty just before it hits the pan. Use about 1 1/2 teaspoons of base per pound of ground beef, start the salt at about 2.7 g per pound and adjust from there. What you should not do is mix salt into the meat before shaping. America’s Test Kitchen found that it turns burgers dense and sausage-like (ATK).

The 6 ingredients of a balanced burger seasoning

A balanced burger seasoning needs just six ingredients. Five are dry spices that keep in a jar. The sixth, salt, goes on at the last minute.

  1. Smoked paprika: color and a gentle smoky note.
  2. Garlic powder: savory depth without burning like fresh garlic.
  3. Onion powder: sweetness and body.
  4. Black pepper: heat and aroma.
  5. Dry mustard: a sharp edge that cuts through the fat.
  6. Salt: added on its own, on the surface, right before cooking.

Most hamburger seasoning recipes online, Well Plated’s and Jessica Gavin’s included, put the salt in the jar. I keep it out, and here is why: once salt is in the blend, you cannot season the meat without salting it too. With a salt-free base, the spices can go into the meat or onto it. The salt waits until the pan is hot.

Why each spice earns its place in the blend

Each spice is there to back up the beef, not to cover it. If the first thing you taste is paprika, the blend is off.

Smoked paprika

Smoked paprika gives the crust its deep red color and a light smoky flavor. That is welcome on a skillet burger that never sees a grill, and it is why I use smoked paprika rather than sweet. One teaspoon of paprika weighs about 2.3 g, according to USDA reference portions.

Garlic powder

Garlic powder holds up on a hot surface. Fresh minced garlic scorches and turns bitter long before the patty is done. One teaspoon weighs about 3.1 g (RecipeTips, USDA data).

Onion powder

You won’t pick out onion powder in a bite. Leave it out, though, and the burger tastes flatter, because it supplies the round, slightly sweet background. One teaspoon weighs about 2.4 g (ReciPal, USDA data).

Black pepper

Grind it fairly fine so it spreads evenly over a thin patty. Coarse cracked pepper lands in pockets. I give no gram weight here because I found no reliable reference weight per teaspoon, so if you cook by the gram, weigh your own.

Dry mustard

This is the spice I would fight for. Dry mustard powder adds a sharp, tangy edge that cuts through the fat of an 80/20 blend, and Well Plated’s hamburger seasoning uses it too (Well Plated). As with pepper, I found no reliable weight per teaspoon, so measure it by the spoon.

Salt

Salt does more for flavor than the other five combined. It is also the one that wrecks the texture if it goes in too early, which is why it gets its own sections below, with a brand conversion table.

Burger seasoning recipe step by step

Mix the five spices once and store them. Then season each patty in two steps: base first, salt last.

1. Measure the spices

Measure 2 tsp smoked paprika, 1 1/2 tsp garlic powder, 1 1/2 tsp onion powder, 1 tsp black pepper and 1/2 tsp dry mustard into a small bowl. Use level spoons, or a scale for the first three.

2. Mix the base

Whisk the spices, or shake them in a small jar, until the color is even with no streaks of paprika. Garlic and onion powder clump; break them up with the back of a spoon.

3. Store the blend

Seal the jar, label it with the date and keep it away from the stove. Well Plated says its blend keeps 6 months in an airtight jar (Well Plated).

4. Season the patties with the base

Use about 1 1/2 tsp of base per pound of beef, a heaping 1/4 tsp per quarter-pound patty. Sprinkle it on both sides or work it in gently while shaping. Either is fine, because the texture problem comes from salt, not from the spices.

5. Salt just before cooking

Pat the patties dry. Salt one side right before it goes into the pan, salted side down, then salt the top just before you flip.

6. Cook to a safe internal temperature

Cook until the center reads 160°F (71°C) on an instant-read thermometer. My guide on how to cook burger patties to a safe 160°F covers the skillet, griddle, grill and smash methods.

How much seasoning per pound of ground beef

Use about 1 1/2 teaspoons of salt-free base and about 2.7 g of salt per pound (453 g) of ground beef. Per quarter-pound patty, that is a heaping 1/4 teaspoon of base and about 0.7 g of salt.

A common benchmark is about 1 tsp of kosher salt and 1/2 tsp of black pepper per pound (Alibaba Spice). Alibaba Spice is a secondary source, though, and it doesn’t say which kosher salt it means. That matters more than it sounds, as the next table shows. Jessica Gavin’s 2 tsp of salted blend per pound comes to about 2/3 tsp of kosher salt (Jessica Gavin). I start at the lighter end and cook a small test patty before seasoning the whole batch.

How to season burgers: when to salt the patties

Salt burger patties on the surface right before cooking, never in the meat ahead of time. Salt mixed in early makes the meat springy and dense. Added at the last minute, it keeps the patty tender and juicy.

What America’s Test Kitchen found

Cook’s Illustrated tested three salting times (ATK):

  • Salt mixed into the beef before shaping: dense and snappy, close to sausage.
  • Patties salted 30 minutes before cooking: dry and springy on the surface.
  • Patties salted just before cooking: the only ones that stayed tender and juicy.

Why salt changes burger texture: myosin

Salt dissolves myosin, a muscle protein, which then forms a sticky gel that binds the ground meat together. Cooked, that gel gives the elastic texture of a sausage (ATK, Food Republic). Great for bratwurst. Wrong for a burger.

My colleague Julia saw it firsthand while developing her Dairy Queen copycat cheeseburger. Her first batch, salted in the mix, came out “tight and a little bouncy”. Salting only the top kept the meat loose.

The one exception: home-ground beef on the grill

ATK makes one exception. If you grind your own beef for the grill, mixing in a little salt before shaping helps the patty hold together on the grate (ATK). For store-bought ground beef in a skillet, salt the surface only.

Burger seasoning FAQ

Here are short answers to the questions readers ask most about seasoning burgers.

What is the best seasoning for burgers?

The best seasoning for burgers is a short blend of smoked paprika, garlic powder, onion powder, black pepper and dry mustard. Salt goes on separately, on the surface, just before cooking.

Should you salt burgers before forming patties?

No. Salt mixed in before shaping dissolves myosin and makes the patty dense and sausage-like. The exception is home-ground beef for the grill, where a little salt helps it hold together (ATK).

How much seasoning per pound of ground beef?

Use about 1 1/2 tsp of salt-free base and about 2.7 g of salt per pound. That is 1 tsp of Diamond Crystal, 1/2 tsp of Morton kosher or a scant 1/2 tsp of table salt.

Do you mix seasoning into burger meat or put it on top?

You can mix the salt-free spice base into the meat or sprinkle it on top. Salt always goes on top, right before cooking.

How long does homemade burger seasoning last?

Kept in an airtight jar away from heat and light, homemade burger seasoning lasts about 6 months, according to Well Plated (Well Plated). Date the label so you know when to make a fresh batch.
